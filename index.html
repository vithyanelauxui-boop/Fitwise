Selling the right part, first time.

An e-commerce concept for an auto care brand that sells spare parts, tyres and tracks for cars, trucks, tractors and construction equipment. This document is the whole trail, not just the screens: how I read the industry, four personas built from real, different contexts, an end-to-end journey map, and the information architecture that came out of all of it — before a single pixel of the Home, Listing and Product pages you can click through below.

The problem

Fitment is the product.

A shirt that's a little big still gets worn. A brake pad for the wrong variant gets returned, after someone has taken the wheel off.

The insight

People shop for a vehicle, not a part.

Nobody knows their variant code or part number, but everyone knows their number plate and what the problem sounds like.

The bet

Vehicle-first, AI-assisted, locally fulfilled.

A garage that travels with you, search that understands symptoms, and dealers who have the part in stock today.

How we'd know

First-time-right fitment.

The one number that captures both the customer's job and the business's biggest cost: returns.

01Reading the brief

It's three screens on paper. Really it's one problem.

The brief: design an e-commerce website for an auto care brand selling spare parts, tyres and tracks for multiple vehicle brands and categories, including cars, trucks, tractors and other vehicles. The PDP has to let someone check compatibility and find local dealers to buy from.

How might we help anyone who looks after a vehicle, from a city car owner to a farmer to a fleet manager, find a part that definitely fits and get it from somewhere nearby, without needing to know a part number?

What “spare parts, tyres and tracks” actually means

These are three catalogues with different shapes. The design has to handle all three without becoming three different websites.

Spare parts

Identified by vehicle + variant + part number

Lakhs of SKUs. Fit depends on make, model, year, engine, and sometimes trim (a disc-brake rear on the top variant only). OE, OES and aftermarket numbers all point to the same physical part.

Tyres

Identified by a size code

205/65 R16 95H. The vehicle only narrows it down: a 2024 Creta SX manual leaves the factory on 16-inch wheels, the automatic on 17-inch. (source) People can also legally go up or down a size. Fit is a range, not a yes/no.

Rubber tracks

Identified by width × pitch × links

300 × 52.5 × 80 for mini excavators and some tractors. High ticket, low frequency, and one link off means it won't tension.

Assumptions I made to confirm with you

India-first, multilingual

Prices in rupees, GST invoices, Indian number plates, Hindi and Tamil alongside English. The pattern travels; the details are local.

Multi-brand, with a dealer network

The brand sells its own range and other brands, and has (or wants) a network of local dealers and garages. Online is the discovery layer; the counter is often where the sale closes.

B2C and B2B on one catalogue

Owners, mechanics and fleet buyers use the same product data but need different views: reassurance for one, speed and credit for the others.

Fitment data exists, or can be built

The whole experience depends on a vehicle-to-part mapping. I treat it as the core asset and design the UI to be honest about where it's uncertain.

02Understanding the industry

It's a huge market, and most of it still runs on the counter.

Before drawing anything I wanted to know how parts actually move in India today, who decides what gets bought, and where the friction is.

₹99,948crore

Indian auto component aftermarket in FY25, about $11.8 billion, up about 6% on the year. (ACMA FY25 review)

10.9lakh

Domestic tractor sales in India in calendar 2025, the first year they crossed one million. Every one of them needs tyres, filters and clutch plates. (TMA data via MaquinAC)

5–40%

Share of fake parts in the aftermarket, depending on who measured and when. The exact figure is disputed; the trust problem isn't. (ACMA study) (2014) (2024)

The person who pays is rarely the person who decides. Any design that speaks only to the owner misses the mechanic, and any design that speaks only in part numbers misses the owner.

Why auto parts are unlike other e-commerce

Fit is binary

Fashion has “roughly right”. Parts don't. A wrong pad is a wasted morning, a return and a lost customer.

The product's identity is hidden

Variant codes, OE numbers and sidewall codes exist, but buyers don't know them. The UI has to translate.

It's a job, not a product

Buying the part is step one. Someone still has to fit it, somewhere, today. Labour and location are part of the purchase.

Three people, one decision

Owner, mechanic and counter all have a say. Each needs different information to say yes.

Downtime costs more than the part

For a farmer at harvest, or a tipper on a quarry contract, speed and certainty beat a ₹200 discount.

Trust is earned slowly

Counterfeits and price opacity mean people default to “my mechanic's guy”. A website has to show its evidence.

What's changing in 2026

Lookup

Number-plate lookups are normal now

Insurance and parts apps have taught people to type a plate and get their vehicle back. A long make/model/year form now feels outdated.

Search

Language models make symptom search viable

“Squeak when braking” can now be turned into a part reliably, as long as a deterministic catalogue makes the final call on fit.

Speed

Quick commerce reset expectations

People expect same-day. Parts can do it through local stock, not new warehouses.

Channel

WhatsApp is the B2B channel

Garages already order from counters on WhatsApp with a photo and a part number. Fit into that rather than fight it.

Language

The next users are vernacular

Tractor owners and small-town garages are the growth segment, and they search in Hindi, Tamil, Telugu and Punjabi, often by voice.

Vehicles

EVs add a new catalogue

Battery, thermal and electronics parts arrive with less familiar names, which makes plain-language search more useful.

04Who we're designing for

Forget demographics. Two questions explained almost everyone.

Demographics didn't separate behaviour usefully. Two questions did: who decides which part to buy? and what does an hour of downtime cost?

Owners need the site to explain: what's wrong, what fits, what it should cost. Professionals need it to get out of the way: part number in, stock and price out, on credit.

Downtime cost changes how much a person will trade price for certainty and speed. A city car can wait till Saturday; a tractor in harvest or a tipper on contract can't.

That gives four proto-personas. They are assumption-based, built from desk research and what I know of the market, not from interviews I haven't been able to run yet — section 12 is how I'd validate them against real people.

06Design principles

Five rules that settled every argument with myself.

Every screen decision was checked against these. When two options looked equally good, the one that served a principle better won.

1

The vehicle is the context

Set it once; every price, badge and filter reads from it. Never make people re-enter it.

2

Fitment is a promise, so show the evidence

Green, amber or red, plus why. Never hide uncertainty to win a sale.

3

Speak human, resolve to part numbers

Accept symptoms, slang, sizes and any language. Translate to the catalogue behind the scenes.

4

Online discovery, local fulfilment

Dealers aren't competitors; they're the last mile, the fitting bay and the credit line.

5

Ask only when it changes the answer

No 9-field forms. A question appears in context, only if the answer changes what fits.

07Information architecture

I built it around a fitment graph. Categories came second.

Most stores start with categories. Here the categories matter less than the relationship between a vehicle and a part, so I started with the data model and built navigation on top of it.

I tried a category-first tree first — Brakes, Tyres, Tracks, Batteries — the way most of the sites in the §03 market scan are built. It fell apart at the very first subcategory: a rear brake disc is a different SKU on a manual Creta than an automatic one, and no category label can carry that distinction. The vehicle-to-part relationship had to be the backbone, with categories sitting on top of it as a filter, not the other way round.

The object model

WhoVehicle

Type → make → model → year → variant. Resolved from a plate, a form or a phrase.

The core assetFitment

Vehicle ↔ OE number ↔ every equivalent part, with a confidence level.

WhatPart

System → category → product → SKU. Tyres add size; tracks add W×P×L.

How muchOffer

Price, stock, ETA, per seller. Online hub and each dealer.

WhereDealer

Location, hours, services (fitting, alignment), credit terms, rating.

Three ways in, one destination

Vehicle-first“My car is a Creta.”

Plate, make/model, or a garage vehicle. Browse by system.

Problem-first“The steering shakes when I brake.”

Natural language, voice or photo. AI maps it to a system and part.

Part-first“51712-C9000” or “205/65 R16”

Part numbers, sizes, track codes. Cross-referenced instantly.

→ A listing filtered to the vehicle

Whatever the entry, people land on the same place: parts that fit their vehicle, with the fitment contract stated at the top.

Decision: AI shows up in four places (search, “Help me choose” on the listing, “Ask about this part” on the product page, and “Customers say” in reviews). All of them open one docked panel: a side panel on desktop, a bottom sheet on mobile. The panel already knows the vehicle and the product, so nobody has to explain their context twice.

Guardrails

AI never claims fit, not even from a photo

Fit badges come only from the fitment database. A photo of a part, label or sidewall only identifies what it might be; it still has to be matched against the customer's actual vehicle, by the catalogue or a person, before anything is called a fit.

Show the working

Parsed chips and reasoning steps let people catch a misunderstanding before they see results.

Say plainly it can be wrong

“Ask Vrom can make mistakes. Suggestions, not a diagnosis” sits under every answer, plus a ₹199 inspection at a partner garage. The AI is a guide, not a mechanic.

Learn from zero results

Unmatched queries feed a synonym list: “liner” for brake shoes, “bush”, regional names for parts.

Grounded summaries only

Quick takes and review summaries are generated from catalogue data and verified reviews, with sources.

Privacy by default

Plates are looked up, not stored, unless the user saves the vehicle. Consent asked in plain words.

  1. One job above the fold: find your vehicle

    A calm studio hero, in the spirit of how Indian car sites present a car: a single-colour headline, the finder on the left, and the official Creta render on the right with three proof points. No auto-rotating carousel (in Baymard's testing, most people scroll past before the slides cycle (source)). The finder with vehicle-type tabs and a number-plate field styled like a real Indian plate (a pattern apps like CRED use for vehicles, here in light mode) that formats itself as you type: TN 09 BX 4821.

    Pattern: number-plate vehicle input · UI Arc Segmented control
  2. One way in without a number: your brand

    The first version had two routes that said the same thing ("Don't know the number? Choose brand and model" and "Or choose your brand"). Now there is one: "No number handy? Pick your brand". The logo row changes with the vehicle type (Maruti, Hyundai, Tata… for cars; Hero, Bajaj, TVS… for bikes; Swaraj, Sonalika, John Deere… for tractors), and tapping a brand moves to model and year, similar to the brand-first fallback on used-car sites like Cars24 and Spinny. "Use number plate instead" appears only inside that step, as the way back.

  3. Say what the number is for, and what the shortcut is

    Two lines used to confuse: "We never store your number" and "Try TN 09 BX 4821". People ask: if you don't store it, why ask? Is that a real vehicle? Whose details will it fill? The shortcut now reads "Use my Creta, TN 09 BX 4821": it is the signed-in shopper's own saved vehicle, one per vehicle type they have, and it is absent for types they haven't saved. The privacy line states the purpose ("We use your number to look up the make, model and variant from the registration record") and makes no promise about storage. In India that lookup typically goes to the national VAHAN registry through a licensed data provider, so the number does leave the app, and signed-in apps usually keep the vehicle in the garage; promising "we never store it" would be a claim the build probably couldn't keep. The result also says where the details came from: "saved in your garage" for the shopper's own vehicle, "from the registration record" for any other number.

  4. My garage, for signed-in users

    A row of vehicle pills switches between saved vehicles. The selected one opens as a single large card, like a car-maker app: the official studio render (360° for the Creta), plate, odometer, last service, and what's coming up, each one tap from shopping.

    The list wasn't wrong, it just asked people to read three lines to find the one that mattered. Putting the same status on the vehicle means the part that's actually due is the thing your eye catches first, not the third row down, and it reuses a mental model every driver already has from their own dashboard. For the business, that's a shorter path from “something's due” to “Shop parts for this car” at the exact moment attention is highest, instead of competing with it.

  5. Categories that don't look like products

    Category tiles use large, angled part renders on warm gradient cards. Product cards sit on plain white with a hairline border, so the two are never confused while scrolling.

  6. Offers as designed cards, not stock photos

    Each offer is a clean tinted card with a product cut-out (discs and pads, a tyre stack, a tractor tyre) and one clear action. They're easy to scan and on-brand, and easy for a marketing team to swap.

    The old layout treated an offer like a category tile: a headline and a paragraph, same weight as anything else on the page. But an offer's only job is to lift cart value, so it has to win the half-second someone spends scanning that row, not read like the rest of the page. Making the number the loudest thing on the card, pairing it with urgency ("Ends Sunday") instead of a sentence, and turning the discount into a code to copy rather than a promise to remember, is what should move it from "noticed" to "used" at checkout. The darker card also reads as a time-boxed promotion rather than another product category, so it doesn't compete with the AI and category tiles above it for the same kind of attention.

10Visual language

It should feel familiar to Indian car buyers and invisible everywhere else.

The palette is almost entirely black and white — closer to Airbnb's neutral ramp than to a typical brand wash — because colour that doesn't mean something is just noise on a page people are scanning for a part number. Real photography and official studio renders carry the visual interest. The only colour that survives is the one red glow on the single primary action per screen, and the three fit signals.

✕ Doesn't fitOnly shown when “Show only parts that fit” is off. Greyed out, never sold by accident.

One primary style: every main action is the red-glow gradient button, secondary actions are white with an outline, and links are plain ink text. That follows Baymard's guidance that buttons and links must never be confused and that colour should carry meaning, not decoration. Because it's the only colour statement on an otherwise black-and-white page, it always reads as “you can act here” without needing a separate brand hue. Green, amber and red are kept apart from it because fitment needs its own clear yes / check / no signal, and they appear only for that.

One AI mark: a soft glowing orb with a sparkle at its centre, wherever Ask Vrom can help (the search bar button, “Ask about this part”, the listing's AI answer, the assistant header) and nowhere else, so it reads as one feature rather than decoration. It animates faster and brighter only while it's actually working on an answer, and freezes under reduced motion.

Accessibility: helper text uses a grey that passes WCAG AA contrast (4.5:1) on white surfaces. Search is a proper combobox: / or ⌘K opens it, arrow keys move through suggestions, Enter opens one, Escape closes it. Search, the assistant and drawers are modal dialogs: focus moves in when they open, Tab stays inside, and Escape returns focus to the button that opened them. Fit states are announced as status messages, and colour is never the only signal (each state also has an icon and a sentence).

Rules I held myself to

One primary action per view

Indigo fills one button per decision. Everything else is an outline or text.

Motion only for feedback

Springs confirm input, shimmer means “working”. Prices and stock never animate. Reduced motion is respected.

Familiar before clever

Rating badges, MRP strike-through, “% off” and sticky mobile buy bars are patterns Indian shoppers already read without thinking.

12What I'd do next

Before I touch pixels again, I'd want these answered.

Future scope · Research

Go where the part changes hands

Shadow two parts counters for a morning. Interview 6 mechanics, 5 owners, 3 farmers (through a dealer) and 2 fleet managers. Test the four proto-personas against reality.

Future scope · Test

Test the three moments

Unmoderated tests on the prototype: find your vehicle, pick a part, reserve locally. Measure time and confidence, not just completion.

Future scope · Data

Audit the fitment data

Sample 200 SKUs and check fitment accuracy by hand. If the data is weak, that's the first product to build, before any UI.

Risks I'm watching

Catalogue quality is the real product

A beautiful fitment card on bad data is worse than none. Confidence levels must be earned per SKU.

Channel conflict with dealers

Online prices and dealer prices must coexist without undercutting the network that does the fitting.

AI overconfidence

Symptom matching is probabilistic. Guardrails, visible reasoning and a human exit are non-negotiable.

Plate lookups and consent

Vehicle registration data is sensitive. Lookups need clear consent, a legitimate data source, and no silent storage.

Questions I'd love to discuss when we meet

  1. Is the brand a manufacturer selling its own range plus others, or a marketplace?It changes how neutral the “best match” ranking can be.
